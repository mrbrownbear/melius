Which AI tool should you use for design concepts?

Choose based on what you're concepting, because the tools that win product design and the tools that win brand direction are barely in the same category. A physical product needs a sketch turned into something buildable. A campaign direction needs a look you can defend in a room and then reproduce. Those are different problems.

The trap in this category is judging tools on whether the output is beautiful. At the concept stage beauty is cheap and abundant. What's scarce is a direction specific enough to brief against, and a look that survives when you apply it to the next twelve assets.

What to know before you pick

  • Concepting and rendering are separate jobs. Exploring twenty directions and resolving one of them reward different tools.
  • A direction you can write down is worth more than one you stumbled into, because only the written one is briefable.
  • You'll generate far more than you present, so how fast you can compare and discard matters as much as output quality.
  • Check whether the concept can travel. A look trapped in one tool's session is a look you'll rebuild later.

What separates a good concepting tool?

Four things, and the last one is the one people discover too late.

How precisely you can set a direction

There's a wide gap between picking minimal from a menu and specifying a warm neutral palette, soft top light from camera-left, matte ceramic surfaces and a shallow depth of field. The second is what lets you match an existing brand or an art director's reference. Judge a tool on how much of that it will actually listen to.

Comparing directions side by side

Concepting is divergent by nature: you make five directions to kill four. A tool that shows you one output at a time forces you to hold the comparison in your head, which is where good options get lost. Being able to branch a direction and see the alternatives together is worth more than a marginally better single image, which is the argument for a node-based creative canvas.

Whether the look survives to the next asset

A concept's job is to be applied. If the approved direction exists only as one lucky image, somebody has to reverse-engineer it for every asset that follows. A look captured as a reusable style, a written description or a saved configuration is one you can still apply in three months.

Getting to something you can present

Concepts get shown to people who didn't make them, which means a raw generation is rarely the deliverable. Mockups, placements, a board that reads as considered: the tools differ a lot on how much of that they'll do, and it's usually the difference between a concept being approved and being misunderstood.

1. Melius

Melius is the Agentic OS for creative work: describe the direction you're chasing and an agent builds the workflow, picks the right model per step, and leaves every step editable. A node-based canvas underneath turns a concept into a graph you can branch, compare and re-run.

That shape suits concepting unusually well. Five directions become five branches off one reference instead of five separate sessions, and the one that gets approved is already a working configuration you point at the next asset.

What it does well

  • Image style analysis reads a reference image and describes its style, which turns somebody's moodboard into something briefable.
  • Sketch to image resolves a rough drawing into realistic detail, so a napkin idea becomes presentable without redrawing it.
  • Brand anchor holds palette, type and rules as a node, so concepts stay inside the brand instead of drifting out of it.
  • Branching on the canvas puts competing directions side by side instead of one after another.
  • Storyboard turns a single image into a 9-panel sequence, which is a quick way to show a direction as a narrative.
  • Multiplayer, so the review happens on the work, not in a separate thread of screenshots.

The style-analysis step is the one that changes the work. Most concepting dies at the handoff, where an approved image has to become a brief that somebody else can execute against. Reading the style out of the reference as text closes that gap: the direction becomes words you can argue with, edit and reapply, not a vibe attached to one file. Behind it sits a library of leading models, so the model that analyses a reference isn't the one asked to render the concept.

The catch: the payoff is in the reuse, so for a single one-off concept you'll feel the setup cost without collecting the benefit.

Best for: brand and campaign concepting where the approved direction has to be applied across a body of work.

2. Midjourney

Midjourney is still the default for visual exploration, and among art directors and brand designers it's the one that gets opened first. Its output has a point of view, which sounds like a criticism and is actually the reason it works for concepting: it proposes things you wouldn't have asked for.

Style references and moodboards have made it considerably more directable than it used to be. You can hand it a set of images and get work in that register, which is the mechanism most concepting workflows now hang off.

What it does well

  • An aesthetic range that proposes directions you would not have thought to specify.
  • Style references let you steer toward an existing look without training anything.
  • Moodboards build a persistent personal aesthetic you can generate against.
  • Genuinely fast divergent exploration, which is the core concepting motion.
  • A very large community, so the craft knowledge is easy to find.

It remains weak at everything after the idea. Precise brand control, legible text, exact palettes and structured output are all outside what it's trying to do, and there's no free tier to test it against your own references first. Concepts also tend to stay in Midjourney: getting an approved look applied to real assets means rebuilding it somewhere else.

The catch: unmatched at proposing directions, unhelpful at executing them.

Best for: the earliest, loosest stage, when you want range rather than control.

3. Vizcom

Vizcom is the tool here built for physical products, and if that's what you design it's not really competing with the rest of the list. It takes an industrial designer's sketch and renders it, keeping the geometry you drew instead of reinterpreting it into something merely similar.

The reason it matters is that product concepting has a downstream that graphics work doesn't. A concept has to become a model somebody engineers, and Vizcom sends work toward computer-aided design instead of stopping at a pretty picture.

What it does well

  • Sketch to render that respects your linework instead of redrawing it.
  • Material and finish exploration on a fixed form, which is exactly how product concepting iterates.
  • 3D output, with a path into Autodesk Fusion for the engineering handoff.
  • Adaptive Canvas removes fixed aspect-ratio limits mid-project.
  • Unify composites a design into a real environment with matched lighting, for validation shots.

Outside physical product design it's the wrong tool, and it doesn't pretend otherwise. There's no brand system, no campaign thinking, and no reason to reach for it if your concepts are graphics, layouts or campaign imagery. Its pricing has also moved around, so check its own pricing page rather than a third-party tracker.

The catch: superb at one discipline, irrelevant to the others.

Best for: industrial, product and packaging designers working from sketches toward something manufacturable.

4. Recraft

Recraft performs best when the concept has to be a design system, not an image. It generates true vectors, editable in Illustrator or Figma, which means a logo or icon concept arrives as something a designer can actually finish, not a raster to trace.

For brand concepting the brand kit is the useful mechanism. Colours, style preferences and identity parameters are stored and applied across everything you generate, so a set of concepts holds together instead of each one being its own accident.

What it does well

  • True scalable vector graphics output, editable downstream in standard design tools.
  • Brand kits that carry colour and identity parameters across a whole set of concepts.
  • Style presets you can define once and reapply, which keeps a system coherent.
  • Strong at icons, illustration and the flat graphic work most AI tools handle badly.
  • A low paid entry point relative to what it does.

It's narrower than it looks. Photoreal imagery, campaign scenes and anything cinematic are not what Recraft is for, and reviewers rate its raster output as unremarkable next to the frontier models. Treat it as the vector and identity specialist, not a general concepting tool.

The catch: brilliant inside vector and brand-system work, ordinary outside it.

Best for: identity, icon and illustration concepts that need to survive into production files.

5. Kosmik

Kosmik is on this list as the honest counterweight: it generates nothing. It's a spatial canvas for visual research, where you collect images, PDFs, video and live web pages, and it surfaces related material and organises what you've gathered.

It earns the slot because a lot of what people call concepting is really research, and doing that badly is why generated concepts come out generic. Feeding a model a considered reference set produces better work than feeding it a sentence, and this is a tool for building the reference set.

What it does well

  • An infinite spatial canvas that holds mixed media, including live web pages, in one place.
  • Surfaces related visual material from something you've uploaded, so research compounds.
  • Auto-organises a growing reference library instead of leaving you to file it.
  • The board itself is the deliverable, which suits a direction review well.
  • A 1-week free trial, enough to build a first board and see whether the habit sticks.

If you need an image made, this is not the tool, and pairing it with a generator is the actual workflow. It's also a smaller product than the rest of this list, which matters if it becomes where your reference library lives.

The catch: it improves the input to concepting rather than doing the concepting.

Best for: art directors and brand designers whose reference libraries have outgrown a folder of screenshots.

From approved concept to finished asset

The handoff is where concepting work usually leaks, and it's worth planning for before you start, not after a direction is signed off.

The failure looks like this: a concept gets approved as a single image, the person who made it moves on, and everyone downstream reverse-engineers the look from that one file. Palette drifts, the type is a near-miss, and three assets in, nobody can say what the direction actually was. The concept was never written down, so it couldn't be applied.

Avoiding it is mostly a matter of capturing the direction as something other than an image: a written style description, a saved style, a brand kit, a configuration you can re-run. Tools differ on whether they let you do that, and it's the criterion that separates a concepting tool from a nice image generator. If a look can only exist as one output, treat the concept as unfinished. The same discipline is what makes marketing image generation survive past the first asset.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best AI tool for design concepts?

Match it to the discipline: Midjourney for the loosest visual exploration, Vizcom for physical product concepts from sketches, Recraft for identity and vector systems, and Melius when the approved direction has to be reapplied across a body of work. Kosmik is the one to add if your problem is research and not generation.

Can AI generate design concepts that are actually usable?

Yes at the direction stage, less so as finished work. These tools are strong at proposing and resolving a look and weak at the precision a production asset needs, so the realistic use is to settle the direction with AI and finish in your normal tools. Treat the output as a concept, which is what it's good at.

How do I keep a concept consistent once it's approved?

Capture the direction as something reusable instead of one image. A written style description, a saved style, or a brand kit can all be reapplied months later; a single lucky generation cannot. On Melius the approved direction stays as a node you re-run against new inputs, which is the same idea made concrete.

Is Midjourney still the best for concept art?

For range and aesthetic quality it's still the one most designers reach for first, and style references have made it much more directable. Its weaknesses are everything after the idea: brand control, legible text, exact palettes and getting a look out of Midjourney and into real assets. There's also no free tier, so you can't test it against your references before paying.

Do I need to be able to draw to use AI concepting tools?

No for graphic and brand concepting, and it helps a lot for product concepting. Vizcom is built around a sketch and rewards being able to make one, while the others take a written direction or a reference set. If you can't draw, working from references instead of from a blank prompt gets you much closer.

Do I own concepts I generate with AI?

It varies by tool, so check before a concept goes into client work. On Melius you own what you create and can use it commercially, subject to the underlying model providers' terms, and Melius does not use your work to train AI models. Read the terms per tool, because free consumer tiers are the ones most likely to restrict commercial use.

Will AI concepting replace art direction?

No. Generating options is now nearly free, which makes choosing between them the scarce skill and no longer the easy part. The judgment about which direction is right for this brand and this audience is the work, and these tools remove the labour around that decision, not the decision itself.
